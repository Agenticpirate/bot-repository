# Taste Compossibility
A taste skill derived from Leibniz's principle of compossibility (Monadology, 1714; Theodicy, 1710): the actual world achieves maximum variety compatible with maximum order. Applied to composition: how much diversity can coexist within a single governing structure without the structure collapsing? The formal signature: multiple simultaneous perspectives on a single subject, fractal self-similarity (the structure of the whole repeated at every scale), and the tension between proliferation and unity.
## Style Tokens
- Maximum variety within unity — abundance governed by a single principle
- Multiple simultaneous perspectives on the same subject
- Fractal self-similarity — the whole's logic visible at every scale
- Proliferation under governance — richness that never becomes chaos
- Each part mirrors whole — microcosm/macrocosm correspondence
- Compossible coexistence — elements that could not exist alone but can together
- Nested structure — hierarchies within hierarchies
- Tension between diversity and order at the breaking point
- Every scale echoes total — zoom in and find the same organizing logic
- Governed abundance — the feeling of plenitude without overflow
## Anti-Tokens
- Monotony — insufficient variety for the governing structure
- Single-perspective dominance — one viewpoint suppressing others
- Arbitrary variety without structure — diversity for its own sake
- Minimalism as reduction — eliminating variety rather than governing it
- Chaos without governance — proliferation that has lost its principle
- Repetition without variation — mechanical copying
- Flatness of scale — no nested hierarchy
- Either/or exclusion — elements that cannot coexist
- Simplification by elimination — achieving order by removing diversity
- Unstructured accumulation — hoarding without organizing
## Application Rules
1. Apply the compossibility test: given the governing structure, how much variety has been achieved without breaking it?
2. Identify the fractal principle: is the organizing logic of the whole visible at smaller scales?
3. Check for multiple perspectives: does the work present its subject from more than one simultaneous viewpoint?
4. Measure tension: where is the point at which one more element would collapse the unity?
5. The best compossible works feel simultaneously abundant and inevitable — nothing could be added or removed
## Evaluation Criteria
- Does the composition achieve maximum variety within its governing structure?
- Is the organizing logic visible at multiple scales (fractal self-similarity)?
- Are multiple perspectives held simultaneously without one dominating?
- Can you sense the tension point — the edge where one more element would collapse unity?
- Does the work feel both abundant and inevitable?
