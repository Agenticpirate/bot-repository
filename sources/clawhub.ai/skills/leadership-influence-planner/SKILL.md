# Leadership Influence Planner

Use this skill to help a leader choose and execute an ethical influence strategy before or during a leadership conversation. The skill is based on Baker's four influence strategies: Investigation, Calculation, Motivation, and Collaboration.

## Boundaries

- Use influence ethically: help people think, decide, or act differently in ways that can benefit them, the team, the organization, or the customer.
- Do not optimize manipulation, coercion, deception, humiliation, or hidden pressure.
- If the user's goal would harm someone, violate consent, evade accountability, or exploit a vulnerability, refuse that part and offer an ethical alternative.
- Distinguish leadership influence from authority: do not recommend threats, forced compliance, or punishment unless the context is legitimate safety, policy, or accountability.
- Preserve agency: good influence gives people enough truth, context, and choice to decide well.

## Core Model

Baker's four strategies combine two axes:

- **Push vs Pull**: Push is direct and assertive; Pull is indirect, invitational, or participatory.
- **Logical vs Emotional**: Logical uses facts, reasoning, tradeoffs, and evidence; Emotional uses values, identity, belonging, energy, story, and care.

The four strategies:

- **Investigation** = Push + Logical. Use evidence, analysis, facts, structure, and a clear argument.
- **Calculation** = Pull + Logical. Help the other person reason through benefits, costs, risks, incentives, and tradeoffs.
- **Motivation** = Push + Emotional. Use vision, urgency, values, confidence, standards, and a direct call to action.
- **Collaboration** = Pull + Emotional. Build trust, listen actively, share ownership, invite ideas, and co-create the path.

## When To Use Each Strategy

### Investigation

Use when:

- The audience respects evidence and competence.
- The issue is technical, operational, financial, legal, or risk-heavy.
- A decision needs clarity, structure, and facts.
- Misinformation or confusion must be corrected.

Avoid or soften when:

- People already feel steamrolled, ignored, or defensive.
- The problem is trust, emotion, identity, fear, or ownership.

Good moves:

- Present the case in 3-5 facts.
- Show cause and effect.
- Name risks and constraints.
- Make a clear recommendation.

### Calculation

Use when:

- The person needs to see "what's in it for me/us."
- You need buy-in without pushing too hard.
- The decision depends on tradeoffs, incentives, or opportunity cost.
- The person is cautious but rational.

Avoid or soften when:

- The conversation needs courage, empathy, or moral clarity more than tradeoff math.
- The person may hear it as transactional or cold.

Good moves:

- Compare options.
- Map benefits, costs, and risks.
- Ask what outcome matters most to them.
- Let them reach the conclusion.

### Motivation

Use when:

- Energy, courage, urgency, morale, or commitment is low.
- The group needs a clear standard, purpose, or call to action.
- The team already trusts you enough to accept a direct emotional push.
- You need to rally people around a meaningful change.

Avoid or soften when:

- The facts are weak.
- People feel manipulated, burned out, or emotionally overloaded.
- The next step is ambiguous.

Good moves:

- Tell the truth about stakes.
- Connect the work to identity, mission, pride, care, or customer impact.
- Use vivid but honest language.
- End with a concrete ask.

### Collaboration

Use when:

- Trust, ownership, morale, or inclusion is the main barrier.
- People have useful local knowledge.
- The decision is complex and implementation depends on buy-in.
- Someone feels unheard or controlled.

Avoid or tighten when:

- Time is short and a decision must be made.
- The issue is already decided by law, safety, budget, or strategy.
- Collaboration would be fake participation.

Good moves:

- Ask open questions.
- Listen for interests behind positions.
- Share ownership of the plan.
- Make the decision boundary explicit.

## Workflow

1. **Clarify the leadership objective**
   - Define the desired outcome in one sentence.
   - Identify whether the leader needs agreement, action, trust, behavior change, information, or conflict reduction.
   - Separate the stated business goal from the interpersonal goal.

2. **Map stakeholders**
   - Identify the person or group to influence.
   - Note their likely incentives, fears, values, authority, constraints, and history with the leader.
   - Estimate current stance: supporter, persuadable, skeptical, resistant, passive, or adversarial.

3. **Diagnose the barrier**
   - Facts barrier: they lack evidence or clarity.
   - Tradeoff barrier: they do not see value or acceptable risk.
   - Energy barrier: they lack urgency, belief, courage, or emotional commitment.
   - Trust/ownership barrier: they feel excluded, controlled, dismissed, or unsafe.

4. **Choose the primary influence mode**
   - Facts barrier -> Investigation.
   - Tradeoff barrier -> Calculation.
   - Energy barrier -> Motivation.
   - Trust/ownership barrier -> Collaboration.
   - If two barriers are strong, sequence strategies instead of blending everything at once.

5. **Plan the sequence**
   - Choose an opener that fits the mode.
   - Choose 2-4 proof points, questions, stories, or tradeoffs.
   - Choose the exact ask.
   - Choose a fallback mode if the first approach fails.

6. **Check for conversation games**
   - If the situation contains recurring circular arguments, blame, rescuing, gotchas, helplessness, or emotional traps, use `transactional-game-analysis` if available.
   - First identify the loop/payoff, then choose the influence mode that exits the loop.
   - Do not pour more persuasion into a game loop; change the transaction first.

7. **Draft the communication**
   - Provide a version for the relevant medium: 1:1 conversation, meeting agenda, Slack/message, email, decision memo, or conflict reset.
   - Keep the user's voice direct, warm, and practical unless they ask for a different tone.
   - Include a short version and a fuller version when useful.

8. **Stress-test the plan**
   - Identify how the other person might resist.
   - Identify what could backfire.
   - Identify what not to say.
   - Adjust for power dynamics, trust, cultural context, and urgency.

9. **Give a next move**
   - End with one recommended first action.
   - Include what success should look like.
   - Include what to switch to if it does not work.

## Strategy Combinations

Use sequences when one mode is not enough:

- **Collaboration -> Investigation**: listen first, then bring structure and facts.
- **Investigation -> Calculation**: prove the case, then help them weigh the tradeoffs.
- **Calculation -> Motivation**: make the rational case, then connect to purpose and action.
- **Motivation -> Collaboration**: rally the team, then invite ownership of execution.
- **Transactional-game reset -> Collaboration**: break the loop before asking for buy-in.

## Leadership Use Cases

Use this skill for:

- Persuading a teammate, partner, client, contractor, collaborator, or vendor.
- Preparing a difficult conversation.
- Getting buy-in for a decision or change.
- Handling resistance without escalating.
- Delegating work with ownership.
- Asking for accountability without sounding punitive.
- Motivating a tired team.
- Aligning people who disagree.
- Turning a vague idea into an influence plan.

## Quality Bar

- Recommend one primary strategy, not all four equally.
- Explain why the chosen strategy fits the stakeholder and barrier.
- Give concrete language the user can actually say.
- Include ethical guardrails and likely backfire points.
- Prefer clear, useful leadership moves over theory explanation.
- When in doubt, lead with Collaboration if trust is low, Investigation if facts are disputed, Calculation if incentives are unclear, and Motivation if energy is low.
