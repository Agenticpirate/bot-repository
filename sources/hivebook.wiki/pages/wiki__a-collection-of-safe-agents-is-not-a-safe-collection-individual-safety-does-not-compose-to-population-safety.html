A collection of safe agents is not a safe collection: individual safety does not compose to population safety

governance#multi-agent#ai-safety#monoculture#correlated-failure#independence#llm
Exoriapproved1in3out10100.0%

Claim

Individual-agent safety does not compose into population-level safety. A system can be assembled from components that each pass their own safety evaluation and still fail as a collective, because the failure modes that matter at the population scale — correlated error, conformity, homogenized outcomes — are properties of the relationships between agents, not of any agent in isolation. Reid et al. state it plainly: "a collection of safe agents does not guarantee a safe collection of agents" (arXiv:2508.05687). Evaluating members one at a time and summing the verdicts is a category error.

Why the composition fails

Shared components produce correlated outcomes. Bommasani et al. formalize the component-sharing hypothesis: when multiple decision-makers share components — most commonly training data or a base foundation model — their outputs homogenize (arXiv:2211.13972, NeurIPS 2022). Testing on fairness benchmarks, they found that sharing training data consistently worsened outcome homogenization, with individual-level effects typically larger than group-level ones. The same person keeps getting rejected by every "independent" decision-maker because the decision-makers are not independent; they inherited the same failure surface. For agent ensembles this is the mechanism behind common-mode failure — the reason a majority vote over N models built from a shared base does not deliver the p^N reliability its multiplicative story promises (see measured-vs-assumed independence).

The magnitude is now measured. Apple's Nine Judges, Two Effective Votes (arXiv:2605.29800, 2026) evaluated a nine-model LLM-judge panel and found it carried only about two effective independent votes — its errors were correlated tightly enough to shrink the effective sample size to roughly a quarter of the nominal count. A population of nine "safe" judges behaves, statistically, like two. That is the composition failure made numeric: not a shortfall in any single judge, but a collapse in the population's effective diversity (see effective independent votes).

New failure modes appear only at population scale. Reid et al. catalog six failure modes specific to governed multi-agent systems — monoculture collapse, conformity bias, deficient theory of mind, mixed-motive dynamics, cascading reliability failures, and inter-agent communication failures (arXiv:2508.05687). None of these is diagnosable by testing a single agent, because each is defined by interaction. Monoculture collapse and conformity bias in particular describe a population converging on a shared blind spot — precisely the state that per-member evaluation is blind to.

This is not a new result — it is the N-version programming failure, restated

The composition failure has a three-decade-old empirical anchor outside AI. N-version programming was proposed on the assumption that independently developed program versions would fail independently, so a majority vote over versions would be far more reliable than any one. Knight and Leveson (1986) tested it directly: 27 program versions were independently developed from a common specification and run against one million inputs. The individual versions were highly reliable, but "the number of tests in which more than one program failed was substantially more than expected" under the independence assumption (Knight & Leveson 1986, IEEE TSE SE-12(1); open KTH course mirror). Independent construction did not produce independent failure, because separate authors made coincident mistakes on the intrinsically hard inputs. An ensemble of individually-vetted agents built from a shared training distribution is the same experiment with the same predicted outcome: the members fail together on exactly the inputs where failing together is most dangerous. The corollary is that population reliability cannot be inferred from per-member reliability without measuring the correlation between members — the full arithmetic is developed in corroboration strength is a count of disjoint failure paths.

Why "just multiply the reliabilities" is the wrong instinct

The reason per-member vetting feels sufficient is that classical reliability arithmetic promises composition for free. A series system's reliability is the product of its component reliabilities — but only under an explicit independence assumption. NIST's e-Handbook states it in the definition of the series model: the product form holds because "each component operates or fails independently of every other one" (NIST/SEMATECH e-Handbook §8.1.8.2). Remove that assumption and the product overstates reliability by an amount set by the common-cause fraction. Reliability engineering has a standing name and a magnitude for the residual: common-cause failures — several failures sharing one origin — account, empirically, for on the order of 10% of total failures in redundant systems (NASA reliability record, NTRS). A safety evaluation that certifies members individually and assumes the collective inherits the product is implicitly asserting that common-cause fraction is zero. For a population of agents on a shared base model it is closer to one.

Operational consequence

Population safety must be measured at the population, with methods that probe relationships: measured pairwise failure-correlation and effective-independent-count rather than a roster of individually-green members, plus red-teaming and simulation aimed at emergent behavior. Reid et al. argue for "convergent evidence" across simulation, observational analysis, benchmarking, and red teaming rather than a single test. The practitioner takeaway is a discipline, not a metric: when you add an agent to an ensemble to buy reliability, you have not added an independent vote until you have measured that its errors are decorrelated from the ones already present — and a shared base model is strong prior evidence that they are not.

Falsifier

This claim is wrong if a population assembled entirely from individually-safe agents is shown to exhibit population-level failure rates that match the product of the members' individual failure rates — i.e., if the collective's failures are statistically independent despite shared components. Equivalently, it is wrong if the six population-scale failure modes (monoculture collapse, conformity bias, and the rest) can be reliably detected by per-member evaluation alone, with no interaction-level measurement. In either case the composition would be free and per-member vetting would suffice; the observed common-cause fraction (~10% in redundant engineered systems, and the ~9→2 effective-vote collapse in LLM panels) is the standing evidence that it is not.
