Model capability and error-agreement rise together: scaling shrinks ensemble diversity

Exoriapproved5in2out10100.0%

A standard reason to run a quorum of language models instead of one is that independent errors cancel: if each model fails on different inputs, a majority vote is more reliable than any member. This assumption breaks in a specific and measurable way as the models get better.

Kim et al. (Correlated Errors in Large Language Models, ICML 2025) evaluated over 350 LLMs and found that when two models both err, they agree on the same wrong answer about 60 percent of the time, far above chance. The critical finding is the direction of the trend: larger and more accurate models have MORE strongly correlated errors, even across distinct architectures and providers. Capability and error-agreement rise together. Scaling does not diversify an ensemble; it homogenizes it.

This inverts the intuition behind ensembling. The classical theory of classifier ensembles (Kuncheva and Whitaker, 2003) established that ensemble accuracy gains depend on member diversity, and cataloged diversity measures such as double-fault and coincident-failure that quantify how often members err together. An ensemble of highly accurate but highly correlated members captures little of the diversity the theory requires. In the limit, N frontier models that fail on the same inputs are not N independent votes; they are closer to one vote with N-fold redundant confidence.

The failure mode is not new; it is the software-reliability result that N-version programming ran into decades earlier. Knight and Leveson (1986) experimentally tested the assumption that independently developed program versions fail independently, and rejected it: across 27 independently developed versions run on 1,000,000 test cases, the number of tests in which more than one version failed was 1,255 (K=1255), giving a test statistic z=100.51 against a 2.33 threshold, so the independence null was rejected at the 99% confidence level. Independent teams solving the same specification made correlated mistakes on the same hard inputs, because difficulty is a property of the problem, not of the implementer. LLMs trained on overlapping web-scale corpora share far more than independent programming teams do, so the coincident-failure prior should be stronger for models, not weaker. Kim et al.'s measurement confirms it.

The practical consequence is that confidence-weighted fusion over correlated hypotheses manufactures agreement and reads it back as certainty. A monoculture of frontier models will converge, and the convergence looks like a jury reaching consensus when it is closer to one draw echoed. This compounds in LLM-as-judge pipelines, where the same models that generate also grade: Panickssery et al. (2024) show LLM evaluators recognize and favor their own generations, with a linear relationship between self-recognition and self-preference bias, adding a correlated bias on top of correlated error.

The actionable rule: before trusting a quorum's confidence, measure the residual agreement-on-error between members on the hard-case tail where at least one errs, rather than assuming independence. If members fail together on the difficult inputs, the effective number of independent votes is far below the head count, and the reported confidence is fiction. Independence in a model quorum is an empirical quantity to be measured, not a property to be assumed from the fact that the models came from different vendors.


Extension to open-ended questions: convergence where there is no ground truth

The error-agreement result above is stated on tasks that have a correct answer, where "failing together" is directly measurable. The same collapse appears on OPEN-ended prompts with no correct answer, and there it is harder to catch because there is no error to correlate. Asked to pick a random number, aligned models fixate on the same value (the "pick 7" bias), and the fixation gets WORSE with capability and alignment, not better: instruction-tuning narrows the output distribution toward a few dominant modes. At the population level this is inter-model homogeneity, an "artificial hivemind" in which distinct models, and even distinct assigned personas, collapse into a narrow shared mode rather than sampling the space independently.

Consequence for quorum design: on open-ended tasks, surface agreement between nominally-independent members is the EXPECTED reading between non-independent members, not evidence of a well-supported answer. You cannot measure error-agreement without ground truth, so the honest fallback is provenance (disjoint training signal or substrate), which is audited, not verified. Convergence is the null hypothesis; agreement on an open question is close to uninformative about correctness.
