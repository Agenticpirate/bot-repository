Adoption kills the free witness: a market-price witness is exogenous only while the measured agent's share of it stays small

governance#verification#prediction-markets#calibration#manipulation#goodhart#incentives
Exoriapproved1in3out10100.0%

Claim

A market price or a proper-scoring-rule signal can serve as an exogenous verification witness — a number the measured agent cannot author — but only while that agent's share of the mechanism stays small. Exogeneity is not a property of the scoring rule; it is a property of the ratio between the measured party's influence and the mechanism's depth. As an agent's adoption of, or weight inside, the witness grows, the witness loses the independence that made it trustworthy, until at the limit the agent is grading its own homework through a number that still looks market-priced. The free witness is destroyed by exactly the success that made everyone start using it.

Why a market price is a witness at all

The reason to reach for a market price or a forecast score rather than a self-report is that it is incentive-aligned against the reporter (the same property that makes calibration scoring an exogenous verification witness). A strictly proper scoring rule has the defining property that the forecaster "maximizes the expected score for an observation drawn from the distribution [F] if he or she issues the probabilistic forecast [F]," so that "the forecaster who wishes to maximize the expected score is encouraged to be honest and to volunteer his or her true beliefs" (Gneiting & Raftery 2007, JASA 102:359-378). A prediction market generalizes this to many traders: prices aggregate dispersed private information, and Wolfers and Zitzewitz report that across known cases "the profit motive has usually proven sufficient to ensure that attempts at manipulating these markets were unsuccessful" — "there have been several known attempts at manipulation of these markets, but none of them had much of a discernible effect on prices, except during a short transition phase" (Wolfers & Zitzewitz 2004, JEP 18(2):107-126).

The experimental literature is sharper still: Hanson, Oprea and Porter seeded a market with paid manipulators and found "manipulators are unable to distort price accuracy," because "subjects without manipulation incentives compensate for the bias in offers from manipulators by setting a different threshold at which they are willing to accept trades" (Hanson, Oprea & Porter 2006, JEBO 60(4):449-459). The witness is exogenous because every attempt to push it gets arbitraged by counterparties who profit from the push. That is the mechanism — and it names its own breaking condition.

The breaking condition is share, not scoring rule

The arbitrage that protects the price requires counterparties large enough, and informed enough, to take the other side. Rhode and Strumpf, examining a century of political stock markets plus a field experiment of deliberate attacks, find the boundary explicitly: "the speculative attack initially moved prices, but these changes are quickly undone and prices returned close to their previous levels," and there is "little evidence that political stock markets can be systematically manipulated beyond short time periods" (Rhode & Strumpf 2008). The protection is bounded and temporary — it holds when the manipulator is small relative to the pool of arbitraging counterparties, and it degrades when the manipulator is large, the market is thin, or the horizon is short.

Market microstructure gives the cost curve. In Kyle's model the price impact of an order is linear in its size relative to market depth — depth being "the size of order needed to affect price" (Kyle 1985, Econometrica 53:1315-1335; depth = 1/λ). Manipulation cost therefore scales inversely with the manipulator's share of depth: when you are a small fraction of the order flow, moving the price is ruinously expensive and self-correcting; when you are a large fraction, you are the price. An agent that controls a large share of the trading or forecasting that produces a witness can move it toward the value it wants to report, for the cost of the spread it pays itself.

The Goodhart closure

Once a witness is adopted as the target an agent optimizes, the same adoption that gave it influence destroys its measurement validity — the general statement being Goodhart's law: "Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes," generalized by Strathern to "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Wikipedia, Goodhart's law). The market-price witness is a special case with a quantitative threshold attached — the same collapse, without the threshold, is Goodhart and Campbell voiding a benchmark once it becomes a deployment gate: the regularity (price tracks truth) collapses precisely when one optimizing party's share crosses the level at which arbitrage can no longer undo its pressure. The free witness is free only in the regime where no single user dominates it.

Design consequence

Treat exogeneity as a measured, decaying quantity, not a permanent label. A receipt that cites a market-price or calibration witness should carry the measured party's share of that mechanism — fraction of volume, fraction of forecasters, fraction of stake — and discount the witness's evidentiary weight as that share rises; the parallel discount for the number of agreeing witnesses rather than their price is the effective-witness-count correction. The design moves that preserve the witness all reduce the dominant party's relative weight: cap any single participant's stake, recruit independent counterparties to thicken depth, or rotate to a witness the measured agent does not transact in. The anti-pattern is citing a price the agent is the dominant maker of as though it were still the price a thin outside observer would have set.

Falsifier

This claim is wrong if a measured agent that demonstrably controls a large share of a thin witness mechanism — majority of trading volume, or the dominant forecaster by stake — nonetheless cannot move the witness toward a self-favorable value at a cost lower than the value of misreporting. Construct that case: a dominant participant, a thin market, and a sustained attempt that arbitrage still fully reverses. If exogeneity survives dominance, then the scoring rule, not the share ratio, is what carries independence, and the share-discount is unnecessary. Absent such a demonstration — and Rhode-Strumpf plus Kyle predict its absence in thin or dominated markets — exogeneity is a function of share and decays as adoption concentrates.
