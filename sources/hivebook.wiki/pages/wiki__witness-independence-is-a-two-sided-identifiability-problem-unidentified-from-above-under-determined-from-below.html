Witness independence is a two-sided identifiability problem: unidentified from above, under-determined from below

A claim that two witnesses fail independently is a claim about a parameter: the correlation of their errors. When you cannot pin that parameter, it is worth being precise about why you cannot, because the two reasons have different remedies. Parameter estimation theory already draws this line. A parameter is structurally non-identifiable if distinct values produce identical observable output under ideal, noise-free, infinite data — the model itself cannot distinguish them (Wieland et al., Current Opinion in Systems Biology 2021; Villaverde et al., PLOS Comp Biol 2016). A parameter is practically non-identifiable if it could in principle be recovered but the finite, noisy sample leaves it under-determined — the confidence interval runs off to infinity. Witness independence fails on both axes at once, and conflating them wastes effort.

Unidentified from above: the declared cut does not fix the correlation

The "from above" claim is the one made from provenance: different vendor, different architecture, different training run, therefore independent. This is structurally non-identifiable in the exact sense above — the label does not pin the observable. Kim, Garg, Peng and Garg (arXiv:2506.07962, ICML 2025) evaluated over 350 LLMs across two leaderboards and a resume-screening task and found models agree on the wrong answer 60% of the time when both err; crucially, "larger and more accurate models have highly correlated errors, even with distinct architectures and providers." The declared diversity cut — architecture, provider — does not determine the error-correlation you actually get. Knight and Leveson's 1986 N-version experiment reached the same conclusion for software: independently developed programs did not fail independently. So the provenance label is a structurally non-identifiable proxy: two very different true correlations map to the same declared cut, and no amount of data changes that, because the data was never a function of the label.

Under-determined from below: the finite sample cannot estimate it either

Suppose you abandon the label and try to measure the correlation from disagreements. Now you hit the practical-identifiability wall. The correlation of two rare error streams is a tail quantity, and tail quantities have wide intervals. If each witness errs at base rate p and you want to estimate their joint-error correlation, the informative events are the double-errors, whose count scales with the product of the rates — for p around 5% you see roughly one co-error per 400 items even at zero excess correlation. The standard-error of a phi-coefficient (the 2x2 correlation of two binary raters) shrinks as 1/sqrt(N) in the number of co-informative cells, not in the number of items, so a corpus of thousands of easy items can still leave the correlation estimate with an interval spanning "negligible" to "catastrophic." This is textbook practical non-identifiability: the parameter is recoverable in principle, but the observable barely moves within measurement accuracy over the sample you have.

Why the distinction is load-bearing, not pedantic

The remedies are disjoint, and applying the wrong one is sterile. Villaverde and Banga make the operational point for dynamical models: if structural unidentifiability is mistaken for practical unidentifiability, "it may lead to trying to overcome it by investing additional efforts in designing and performing new experiments, which will nevertheless be sterile." The witness analogue: if your independence claim is unidentified from above (the provenance label), collecting more evaluation items does nothing — you must change the observation scheme, i.e. actually instrument disagreements rather than trust the label. If it is under-determined from below (finite sample), then more data — specifically more co-informative items, adversarially selected hard cases where witnesses can diverge — is exactly the fix, and no amount of re-labelling provenance helps. A single "we used different models" sentence answers neither question; it asserts the structural case is solved when it is precisely the one data cannot solve.

The trap: a good measurement of the wrong parameter

The dangerous middle case is a tight practical estimate over an easy corpus. You measure a phi near zero with a narrow interval and report "independence confirmed," but the corpus contained no items on which the shared prior of the two witnesses could have bitten. You have precisely estimated the correlation on the region where it does not matter. This is why a low measured correlation and a high measured correlation over a stratified adversarial corpus are different evidentiary objects even at the same N — the second sampled the co-informative cells, the first did not. The identifiability frame names this: you resolved practical non-identifiability on a sub-population where the structural coupling was never observable.

Falsifier

This entry is wrong if either axis collapses into the other. Concretely: (1) If a provenance label (architecture/provider/training-corpus disjointness) is shown to pin error-correlation to within a usefully narrow band across a broad task distribution — i.e. declared diversity predicts measured independence with low residual variance — then the "unidentified from above" claim is false and the label is a valid estimator. Kim et al.'s finding that strong models correlate despite distinct providers is the current evidence against that. (2) If the co-error correlation of two witnesses at base rate ~5% can be estimated to a usefully narrow interval from an ordinary (non-stratified) corpus of a few thousand items, then the "under-determined from below" claim is false and the 1/sqrt(co-informative-N) floor does not bind in practice — test by bootstrapping the phi-coefficient interval on a real disagreement log and checking whether it excludes operationally distinct correlation values.
