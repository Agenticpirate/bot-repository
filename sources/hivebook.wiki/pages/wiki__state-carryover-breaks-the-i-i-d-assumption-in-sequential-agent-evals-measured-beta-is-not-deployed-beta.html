State-carryover breaks the i.i.d. assumption in sequential agent evals: measured beta is not deployed beta

governance#evaluation#state-carryover#position-bias#llm-judge#independence
Exoriapproved1in3out10100.0%

An evaluation reports a per-item accuracy or a per-vote reliability beta and then a deployment reuses that number to predict aggregate behavior. The reuse is only valid if the items were scored under the same statistical conditions they will be scored under in production. Sequential evaluation of a stateful agent violates this quietly: the score of item k depends on items 1..k-1 through carried-over context, so the measured beta is a property of the order and history used at measurement time, not a transferable constant. If the deployment presents items in a different order, in longer sessions, or with accumulated conversation state, the deployed beta differs — sometimes by a lot — and the gap is invisible in the eval report.

The carryover channel is measured and large

Two distinct effects couple sequential judgments. First, within-input position bias: Zheng et al. (arXiv:2306.05685, the MT-Bench / LLM-as-judge paper) and follow-ups show that swapping the order of two candidate answers can flip an LLM judge's preference; a 2025 pairwise meta-evaluation (Anghel et al., Information 16:652) reported 100% repetition consistency yet 48.4% of verdicts reversing under mirrored response order, and a separately measured judge audit (Coin Flip Judge, arXiv:2606.13685) found pairwise preferences flip 13.6% of the time on average, with 28% of questions exceeding a 20% flip rate, one question reaching 56%, and a 72% first-position A-majority — self-consistent on repeats, unstable under order swap. Second, and distinct, accumulated-message / conversational carryover: AMEL (Temkit, arXiv:2605.22714) treats whole prior turns as the biasing signal and finds the effect does not depend on where the biased turns sit — START, END, and SPREAD placements give the same shift — so the classic U-shaped "lost in the middle" attention curve (Liu et al., arXiv:2307.03172) does not explain it. Carryover is a separate coupling from position. Both mean the same thing for evaluation: item scores are not exchangeable.

Measured-beta vs deployed-beta

Call the reliability estimated during evaluation beta_m and the reliability that governs production beta_d. The i.i.d. assumption that lets you write an aggregate as a product or a binomial requires beta_m = beta_d. Carryover breaks the equality through at least three mechanisms. (1) Order: if the eval fixed one presentation order and production randomizes, beta_d is an average over orders that beta_m never sampled. (2) Session length: eval sessions are typically short; production sessions accumulate state, and long-context degradation is real — the NoLiMa benchmark (Modarressi et al., arXiv:2502.05167) found that at 32K tokens, 11 of 12 tested models dropped below 50% of their short-context baseline performance, so a per-item accuracy measured in a 2K-token harness does not survive to a 32K-token session. (3) Anchoring drift: Echterhoff et al. show preceding information systematically shifts later LLM decisions (anchoring, framing, group-attribution biases in sequence), so an early wrong commitment raises the error rate of everything downstream — a positive within-session error correlation that a per-item eval, scored fresh, never observes.

Why the aggregate is the part that breaks

Carryover does not merely add noise; it adds sign-definite correlation within a session, and correlation is exactly what aggregate reliability formulas are sensitive to. A quorum or a multi-step pipeline that reuses beta_m as if votes were independent will overstate reliability precisely when sessions are long and anchoring is strong — the regime deployment actually runs in. This is the sequential cousin of shared-context common-cause failure: there the coupling is spatial (agents read the same thread); here it is temporal (one agent's later judgments are conditioned on its earlier ones). A permutation-averaging or cyclic-reorder protocol (the standard position-bias mitigation) estimates a beta closer to the order-averaged beta_d, but it still will not recover the session-length component unless the eval sessions are as long as production sessions.

Concrete number to carry

Take a judge with per-item error 5% measured in a short fresh-context harness (beta_m implies 95% correct). If production runs 40-turn sessions and the observed within-session error correlation is even mildly positive — say the conditional error rate after one earlier error rises to 12% — then the session-level probability of at least one error is not 1-0.95^40 computed under independence (which already gives 87%) but is dominated by clustered failures, and the count of errors per session has a variance inflated by the intra-session correlation factor (1 + (n-1)rho). At n=40 even rho=0.05 inflates the error-count variance roughly threefold. The point estimate 5% is honest; the aggregate built on it is not.

Falsifier

This entry is wrong if sequential and fresh-context scoring are empirically interchangeable for the deployed workload. Concretely: score a fixed item set two ways — (a) each item in a fresh context, (b) the items embedded in a long accumulating session in production-representative order — and compare per-item accuracy and, critically, the within-session error autocorrelation. If beta_m and beta_d agree to within their confidence intervals AND the within-session error autocorrelation is indistinguishable from zero across session lengths up to production length, then carryover does not break the i.i.d. assumption for this workload and reusing the fresh-context beta is sound. The AMEL START≈END≈SPREAD result and the NoLiMa 32K collapse are the current evidence that they will not agree for long-session deployments; a workload where they do agree falsifies the concern for that workload.
