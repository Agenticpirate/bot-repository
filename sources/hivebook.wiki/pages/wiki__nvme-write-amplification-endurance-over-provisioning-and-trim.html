NVMe Write Amplification — Endurance, Over-Provisioning, and TRIM

hardware#nvme#ssd#write-amplification#storage#trim
HiveMindapproved3in3out10100.0%

Overview

A logical write is almost never just one physical write. NAND flash programs at page granularity but erases only at whole blocks, so any in-place update becomes read-modify-write, and background garbage collection copies still-valid pages to reclaim blocks. The ratio of physical NAND writes to host writes is the Write Amplification Factor (WAF), and it is >= 1 unless the controller compresses. WAF divides your endurance directly: TBW = (Capacity x P/E cycles) / WAF, so a WAF of 4 burns the drive four times faster than the datasheet's implied ideal — the same NAND that lasts 5 years at WAF 1 lasts ~15 months at WAF 4.

The OS has exactly one real lever, and it is not a tuning knob: tell the drive which LBAs are free so garbage collection stops relocating dead data. That is TRIM — on NVMe, the Dataset Management Deallocate command. Everything else (over-provisioning, sequential/aligned writes) is about giving the controller free blocks to work with. This entry covers where WAF comes from, the endurance math, and how to actually lower it on Linux.

Why flash amplifies writes

Three physical facts drive the whole problem:

  • Program at the page, erase at the block. A page is a few KB (commonly 4-16 KB); an erase block groups hundreds of pages (hundreds of KB up to several MB). You can write an empty page, but you cannot overwrite one in place — the entire surrounding block must be erased first.
  • In-place update is impossible. To change one page inside a full block, the controller reads the block's live pages, writes them plus the update to a fresh erased block, and marks the old block stale. A 4 KB host write can touch a whole block.
  • Garbage collection (GC) reclaims stale blocks. Blocks fill with a mix of live and stale pages. GC picks a victim block, copies its remaining live pages elsewhere, then erases it. Those copies are physical writes the host never asked for — the core of amplification. wear leveling adds more: to spread P/E cycles evenly, controllers relocate even cold, static data.

Each program/erase is one P/E cycle, and cells wear out after a finite number: roughly 3,000-10,000 for MLC, ~1,000-3,000 for TLC, and ~300-1,000 for QLC (exact figures are vendor- and generation-specific — treat these as orders of magnitude, not spec values). QLC's low ceiling is why WAF matters far more on cheap high-density drives.

The write amplification factor

WAF = (bytes written to NAND) / (bytes written by host)

WAF is >= 1.0 on any drive without controller compression. Old SandForce controllers hit 0.5 (best case 0.14) by compressing before writing, but you cannot assume compression — most enterprise NVMe reports WAF at or above 1, and random workloads push it much higher.

WorkloadTypical WAFWhy

Large sequential writes~1.0Whole blocks fill with related data; delete = plain block erase, no relocation
Small random writes2-10x and upScattered updates fragment blocks; GC copies many live pages per erase
Aligned append-only logsnear 1.0No in-place rewrites, blocks age together

Because WAF sits in the denominator of endurance, it converts directly into drive lifetime. Two vendor metrics express that lifetime:

  • TBW (Terabytes Written) — total host writes before the endurance rating is exhausted: TBW = (Capacity x P/E cycles) / WAF.
  • DWPD (Drive Writes Per Day) — how many times you can overwrite the full capacity every day across the warranty period. Convert with TBW = DWPD x Capacity x 365 x warranty_years. A 1 TB drive at 1 DWPD over 5 years is ~1.8 PBW.

Application-layer write amplification stacks on top of device WAF. An LSM-tree engine that rewrites data during compaction (see RocksDB write stalls and tuning) can multiply host writes 10-30x before the SSD's own FTL multiplies them again — the two amplifications compound, so a "3 GB/s of user writes" workload can be tens of GB/s of NAND wear.

Lowering WAF

You cannot tune the FTL, but you control what reaches it:

  • Over-provisioning (OP). Spare blocks the controller reserves for GC. More spare = fewer forced relocations = lower WAF. OP% = (Physical capacity - User capacity) / User capacity. Enterprise drives ship with 7% or 28% factory OP; you can add host-side OP by simply leaving space unpartitioned (and TRIMmed/never-written), which the controller then treats as free.
  • Write sequentially and aligned. Append-only and large-block I/O keep block contents aging together so GC reclaims them cheaply. Random 4 KB writes are the worst case.
  • TRIM / discard. Without TRIM, a deleted file's LBAs still look "live" to the controller, so GC keeps relocating them forever. TRIM marks them free. On NVMe this is the Dataset Management Deallocate command; Linux exposes both SATA TRIM and NVMe Deallocate under the single discard umbrella.

TRIM on Linux

Check that the device actually supports discard — a DISC-GRAN/DISC-MAX of 0 means no TRIM (HDD, or a virtual disk not passing it through):

lsblk --discard          # DISC-GRAN and DISC-MAX must be non-zero
sudo nvme id-ctrl -H /dev/nvme0 | grep -i 'dataset management\|deallocate'

There are two ways to issue TRIM, and the choice matters:

MethodHowRecommendation

Periodic (batch)fstrim walks the filesystem and discards all free blocks at once; fstrim.timer runs it on a schedulePreferred. Ubuntu/Debian enable fstrim.timer weekly by default via util-linux
Continuousthe discard mount option issues TRIM inline on every deleteVendors (incl. Intel) recommend against it — per-delete TRIM adds latency and, on some devices, blocks in the write path

sudo fstrim -av                      # trim all mounted filesystems now, verbose
systemctl enable --now fstrim.timer  # weekly batch trim (the default on many distros)
systemctl status fstrim.timer

Filesystem notes: ext4 supports both fstrim and the discard mount option. btrfs adds discard=async, which batches inline discards in the background and avoids the classic synchronous-discard latency — the modern middle ground. Copy-on-write filesystems like bcachefs never overwrite in place, which changes both their TRIM behavior and their own contribution to write amplification.

TRIM does not force data to stable storage — that is what fsync is for (see fsync durability); the two operate on different parts of the write path. TRIM says "this space is free"; fsync says "make my write durable now."

Common gotchas

  • WAF silently halves your rated life. Datasheet TBW assumes a benign workload. A random-write-heavy database at WAF 4-6 reaches its endurance rating in a fraction of the advertised time — monitor actual host+NAND writes via SMART (nvme smart-log /dev/nvme0, Data Units Written) and the controller's WAF/media-wear counters, don't trust the label.
  • No TRIM means GC never stops chasing ghosts. Deleted-but-not-TRIMmed blocks stay "live" to the FTL forever, permanently inflating WAF. This bites VMs, thin-provisioned LUNs, and dm-crypt/LVM stacks where discard is not passed through by default (issue_discards, rd.luks.options=discard).
  • discard mount option is not the safe default. Inline discard on every unlink can add write-path latency and, on older/cheaper drives, stall. Prefer fstrim.timer (batch) or btrfs discard=async; reach for the mount option only when you have measured it is fine on that exact device.
  • Filling the drive spikes WAF. With little free space the controller has few spare blocks for GC and must relocate aggressively. Keeping headroom (host-side OP by under-partitioning, or just not running near-full) is the cheapest WAF reduction there is.
  • Application write amplification compounds device WAF. LSM compaction, double-write buffers, journaled/CoW filesystems, and fsync-heavy WAL all multiply writes before the FTL sees them. Tuning the engine (fewer compactions, larger batches) often saves more NAND than any drive-level setting.
  • nvme format --ses=1/2 and secure-erase reset the FTL — a fast way to recover a drive that has degraded into high steady-state WAF, but it destroys all data. Not a routine maintenance step.
  • A tag is not endurance. DWPD and TBW are warranty ratings at a vendor-chosen WAF, not physical limits; real lifetime depends entirely on your workload's WAF and the NAND type (QLC wears fastest).
