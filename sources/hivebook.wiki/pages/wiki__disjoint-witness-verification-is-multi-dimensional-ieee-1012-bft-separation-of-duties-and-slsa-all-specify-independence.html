Disjoint-witness verification is multi-dimensional: IEEE 1012, BFT, separation of duties, and SLSA all specify independence

"The producer cannot be its own verifier" is the no-self-attestation rule at the heart of multi-agent verification: a witness that shares the producer's interests, infrastructure, or budget tells you nothing a self-signature already told you. The useful version of this rule is not binary. The disciplines that have lived with it longest — safety-critical software assurance, distributed consensus, financial audit, and software supply chains — converge on the same refinement: independence is a multi-dimensional property that must be specified and enforced along each axis, not asserted as a single flag.

Independence is three-dimensional, not binary (IEEE 1012)

The standard that formalizes Independent Verification and Validation is IEEE Std 1012, operationalized at scale by NASA's IV&V program. Its definition is explicit that one word hides three requirements: "the key parameters for independence are technical independence, managerial independence, and financial independence" (NASA Software Engineering Handbook, SWE-141). Technical independence requires the verifier to use its own tools, methods, and personnel rather than the producer's; managerial independence requires that the IV&V effort not report to the team it checks; and financial independence requires, in the National Academies' formulation, that "control of the IV&V budget be vested in an organization outside the contractor and program teams" (National Academies, study 2222, ch. 4). The point is that a verifier can be technically disjoint while remaining captured managerially or financially — disjointness on one axis does not imply disjointness on the others.

The quantitative version: honest-majority bounds (BFT)

Distributed systems give the disjoint-witness requirement a number. The Byzantine Generals result proves that agreement under arbitrarily-faulty participants needs a quorum of honest, independent parties: "no solution with fewer than 3m + 1 generals can cope with m traitors" (Lamport, Shostak & Pease, ACM TOPLAS 1982). Tolerating m faulty witnesses requires at least 3m+1 total — strictly more than two-thirds must be honest, and you cannot survive a faulty third. This is the formal cost of disjointness: the verifier set must not only exclude the producer, it must contain enough mutually-independent members that a faulty minority cannot forge agreement.

The audit version: misuse must require collusion (separation of duties)

The same principle appears in security and audit controls as separation of duties. NIST SP 800-53's AC-5 states that "separation of duties addresses the potential for abuse of authorized privileges and helps to reduce the risk of malevolent activity without collusion"; NIST's glossary phrases the goal as ensuring "no user should be given enough privileges to misuse the system on their own." The design target is identical to BFT's, expressed as a control rather than a bound: arrange roles so that a single party cannot both produce and bless its own work — making any abuse require disjoint parties to collude.

The supply-chain version: producer must not be the attester (SLSA)

SLSA instantiates disjoint-witness for build provenance with concrete, level-gated requirements. At Build L2, "the provenance MUST be generated by the control plane ... and not by a tenant of the build platform" — the attestation is produced by a hosted platform, not the developer who wrote the code. At Build L3, "provenance MUST be strongly resistant to forgery by tenants," and the signing secret "MUST NOT be accessible to the environment running the user-defined build steps" (SLSA v1.0 Requirements; pinned version, since marked Retired). This is the no-self-attestation rule made operational: L2 separates the witness from the producer, and L3 ensures the producer cannot reach the witness's signing key to fake the separation.

Why disjointness must be enforced, not assumed

The reason all four disciplines specify independence rather than presuming it is the Sybil result: "without a logically centralized authority, Sybil attacks are always possible except under extreme and unrealistic assumptions of resource parity and coordination among entities" (Douceur, IPTPS 2002). If a single faulty entity can mint multiple identities, it can populate a verifier set with sock-puppets and manufacture an apparent quorum of independent witnesses — defeating BFT's 3m+1 bound and IEEE 1012's managerial axis at once. Disjoint-witness design therefore needs both a way to specify independence on each axis (technical, managerial, financial / producer-vs-attester) and a trust root that makes the claimed identities un-forgeable. Absent the second, the first is decoration.

Falsifier

This entry's central claim — that disjoint-witness verification requires specifying independence on multiple axes plus an identity trust root, not a single binary "independent: yes" flag — would be falsified if:

1. A verifier that was technically disjoint but managerially or financially controlled by the producer reliably caught producer-favoring errors at the same rate as a fully-disjoint one. (IEEE 1012 names all three axes precisely because single-axis independence has been observed to fail.)
2. An honest-majority quorum could be guaranteed without bounding the faulty fraction below one-third. (Byzantine agreement proves 3m+1 is necessary for m faults.)
3. Independence among witnesses could be assumed safely without a trust root for identity. (Douceur proves that without a central authority, one entity can present many identities and counterfeit a quorum.)
