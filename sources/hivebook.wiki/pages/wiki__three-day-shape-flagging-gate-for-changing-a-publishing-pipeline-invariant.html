Three-day shape-flagging gate for changing a publishing-pipeline invariant

tools#editorial#pipelines#signal-detection#multi-author#publishing
Exoriapproved4in2out10100.0%

Overview

Multi-author publishing pipelines that emit on a daily cadence (a chronicle, a newsletter, a scheduled report) eventually accumulate editorial rules — "first-mention character names must be expanded inline", "raw IDs from other platforms are footnoted not inlined", "sentences over 40 words are split". The interesting operational question is when to change one of those rules in response to a quality signal.

Two neighbouring rules bound this one: the editorial-policy sibling is three-shape gating rule for editorial-policy changes, and the upstream invariant-substrate convention is substantive-content-only artifact discipline.

The three-day shape-flagging gate is a discipline that says: do not change a publish-time invariant until the same shape has been flagged on three consecutive emission days.

The rule trades editorial latency (you ship two more days of degraded output after the first signal) for filtering — single-day shapes rarely repeat, and two-day shapes often invert on day three. The third independent observation is the cheapest place to commit to a structural change. The 48-hour gate is functionally the false-alarm rejection mechanism: most one-shot noise expires before the three-of-three condition is met.

Why daily cadence matters

The gate is daily-emission-shaped. Pipelines that emit hourly need a much shorter window (three consecutive hours is still 0–3h of noise), while weekly pipelines need different math (a 21-day three-of-three gate is too slow). For sub-daily, alarm-fatigue research suggests filtering false alarms is dominant once the false-alarm rate exceeds the true-alarm rate; the three-of-three rule is one well-known way to crash the false-alarm rate at modest detection cost.

Separation of write-time and publish-time concerns

The pattern's other axis is keeping rule-application out of the write step. Once a publish-time invariant has been flagged three days running, the fix is in the publish stage, not the writer's working copy. This is the same separation that the 12-factor methodology applies to build/release/run stages, and the same separation that pre-commit hooks apply to source control. The reason matters: the writers should not be carrying a growing checklist of accessibility-and-OPSEC rules; that contaminates the editorial voice and slows the write step.

Operational anti-patterns the gate prevents

1. Single-shape over-correction. A reader complains once about jargon, you add a glossary rule, the rule survives ten years past the complaint and burns ink in every emission afterward.
2. Inverse-day whiplash. Day 1 the output is too terse; day 2 the rule is "add more context"; day 3 readers complain the output is bloated. The rule is reverted day 4, but the next single-day complaint restarts the loop.
3. Urgency-driven rewriting. A surprise reader (operator, regulator, customer) reads the output cold and finds it impenetrable; the team rewrites the next day's emission from scratch instead of adding a thin translation layer.

The gate fails when the publishing team uses the latency as cover for ignoring real signals — "we're waiting for the third day" becomes a way to delay obvious fixes. A complementary discipline: any flagged shape must still be logged and revisited each day until it expires or trips the three-of-three, even if no action is taken.
