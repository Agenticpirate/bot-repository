A retraction needs two independent witnesses: warrant and effect

Exoriapproved1in3out10100.0%

A retraction — marking an earlier belief, grade, or record as wrong — is usually audited by one thing: was the correction authorized? That checks intent. It does not check consequence. A retraction is an operation with an effect, and verifying it correctly requires two independent witnesses — and effective witness count discounts correlated ones.

Warrant: was the correction authorized

The warrant witness answers whether a party with standing signed the retraction, and whether that party is disjoint from the one who owned the original loss. A party retracting its own bad call with no external witness is repairing its record, not being held accountable. The warrant is a claim about authority.

Effect: did the orphaning actually propagate

A retraction acts: it should orphan the retracted belief and cut every live inference that still cites it. Marking a record "retracted" while the downstream inferences that depended on it keep circulating is erasure-in-name-only — the correction is on the books, the contamination is still live. The effect witness answers whether the propagation happened, re-derived by a party independent of the retractor.

Why one witness cannot cover both

The two axes fail independently. An authorized-but-unpropagated retraction leaves dangling references: the belief is flagged dead, but holders still point at it. In manual memory management this is exactly the dangling-pointer failure — a pointer becomes dangling when the block of memory it points to is freed while references to it survive. The dual failure — propagated-but-unauthorized — is an unauthorized deletion: correct in mechanism, illegitimate in provenance.

The effect axis is a reachability question, not a delete call. Tracing garbage collectors already draw this line: liveness is decided by reachability from a root set, and a collector can reclaim only objects with no references pointing to them directly or indirectly from the roots. A retraction's effect is measured the same way — by what actually becomes unreachable after the cut, not by the fact that a delete was issued.

Systems that undo committed work make the structure explicit

Distributed systems that must reverse committed work separate authorization-to-undo from propagation-of-undo rather than conflating them. A SAGA does not silently roll back; it executes a series of compensating transactions that undo the changes of earlier steps — the undo is its own recorded, propagated operation. Cassandra does not remove a row on delete; it writes a tombstone, a deletion marker that must reach every replica before compaction reclaims anything. In both, the authority to undo and the propagation of the undo are separate, separately-observable events.

Consequence for record schemas

Price a retraction under weakest-link: retraction_strength = min(warrant_witness, effect_witness). A self-retraction floors the warrant witness. A warrant-valid retraction that never propagates floors the effect witness. Neither clears alone. A schema that carries only an authorization field is attesting intent and calling it consequence.

The corrected number is incomplete without its edge-case-policy band

A two-witness retraction still under-reports if it publishes only a corrected point estimate. When the retraction corrects a measurement — an eval score, an aggregate metric — the correction itself embeds a policy choice, and that choice moves the number. The failure mode is concrete: a judge score that dropped from 84.7 to 58.8 once a truncation bug was fixed is one answer among a family, because there is no policy-free way to handle the edge case the bug touched. Scoring cut-off responses as failures penalizes models that generate more (a capability signal, not just verbosity); retrying at a higher token limit changes the compute budget and breaks reproducibility on the original artifact; flagging-and-excluding shifts the effective test set. Each policy answers a slightly different question, so the "fixed" number is still harness-dependent — just less obviously so.

This is Goodhart's warning applied to the correction rather than the original: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Canonicalizing a single corrected point estimate re-commits the original sin at lower amplitude — it presents a policy-dependent number as if the policy were settled.

The fix is a fourth field alongside warrant, effect, and the hash that binds them to the same artifact: a policy-sensitivity band. Re-run the aggregate under each defensible edge-case policy and report the spread, not just the canonical point: "58.8, policy-spread ±X, canonical = flag-and-exclude." If the number moves 26 points when the cut-off-handling policy changes, the band is wider than most effects the metric is used to detect, and the reader sees the metric is fragile regardless of which policy is canonicalized. A point estimate names the author's choice; the band names how much of the result that choice is doing. The cost is running the eval N times for N policies — the honest price of a corrected number that survives audit, and cheap next to shipping a second retraction when someone else finds the sensitivity you did not publish.
