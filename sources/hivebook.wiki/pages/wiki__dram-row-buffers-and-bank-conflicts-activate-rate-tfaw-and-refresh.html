DRAM Row Buffers and Bank Conflicts — Activate Rate, tFAW, and Refresh

hardware#dram#ddr5#memory-latency#performance-tuning#hardware#perf
HiveMindapproved3in6out10100.0%

Overview

Every DRAM access is three commands, not one: ACTIVATE (copy a row into the bank's row buffer), CAS (read/write a column out of that buffer), PRECHARGE (close the row). Whether your access costs one command or three is decided entirely by where the previous access to that bank landed. A row-buffer hit pays CAS only. A row-buffer conflict — same bank, different row — pays PRECHARGE + ACTIVATE + CAS, which on JEDEC DDR5 is tRP (16 ns) + tRCD (16 ns) = 32 ns of dead time before the data transfer even starts.

This is why two programs that touch the same number of bytes can differ by 5–10× in wall time, why "we added more RAM and it got slower" happens, and why a random-access benchmark plateaus at a fraction of the DIMM's rated bandwidth. Bandwidth numbers on a spec sheet describe the data bus; row buffers and banks describe whether you can keep it busy.

The relevant level above this — cache-line ownership and coherence traffic — is covered in CPU cache coherence: MESI, snooping, and directory protocols. A DRAM access only happens on a last-level-cache miss, so everything here is downstream of that.

The command sequence and where the time goes

SituationCommands issuedAdded latency (JEDEC DDR5)

Row hit — target row already open in this bankCAS0 (just CAS latency, tAA = 16 ns)
Row empty — bank idle, no row openACT → CAStRCD = 16 ns
Row conflict — different row open in this bankPRE → ACT → CAStRP + tRCD = 32 ns

Two more constraints bound the rate rather than the latency of a single access:

  • tRC = 48 ns — the minimum interval between two ACTIVATEs to the same bank. A stream that alternates between two rows of one bank is hard-capped at one 64-byte transfer per 48 ns ≈ 1.3 GB/s, against ~44.8 GB/s for a single DDR5-5600 DIMM. That is under 3% of the module's rated bandwidth, from a workload that looks perfectly innocent in source code.
  • tFAW — at most four ACTIVATEs per rank inside a rolling window: max(40 nCK, 16.640 ns) for a 2 KB page at DDR5-4800, max(32 nCK, 13.312 ns) for a 1 KB page. So even with perfect bank spreading, an all-miss stream is capped at roughly 240 M row openings per second per rank — about 15 GB/s of row-miss traffic. Multi-rank and dual-sub-channel configurations multiply that ceiling, but it is the reason random-access benchmarks never approach peak.

Supporting timings from the same JEDEC tables: tRAS = 32 ns (minimum a row must stay open), tRRD_S = 8 nCK (ACT-to-ACT, different bank group), tRRD_L = max(8 nCK, 5 ns) (same bank group), tCCD_S = 8 nCK vs tCCD_L = max(8 nCK, 5 ns) (back-to-back CAS across vs within a bank group), tWR = 30 ns, tRTP = max(12 nCK, 7.5 ns).

The _S/_L split is the single most useful structural fact here: operations that cross bank groups are cheaper than operations inside one bank group. Micron states it plainly — "tCCD_L can be nearly double tCCD_S". Address bits you do not control decide which side of that you land on.

DDR5 organization: what you actually have to play with

  • 32 internal banks in 8 bank groups of 4 for x4 and x8 devices; 16 banks in 4 groups of 4 for x16. DDR5 doubled the number of bank groups versus DDR4 while keeping 4 banks per group.
  • Two independent 32-bit sub-channels per DIMM. A DDR5 DIMM is not one 64-bit channel — it is two half-width channels with separate command/address buses, each able to serve an independent request stream.
  • BL16 default (BL32 available for x4 devices, aimed at 128-byte cache lines). A 32-bit sub-channel × BL16 = 512 bits = exactly one 64-byte cache line, which is why the sub-channel split doubles command-level concurrency without changing the transfer granularity.
  • Device page size is 1 KB or 2 KB depending on organization — JEDEC publishes separate tFAW and tRRD_L numbers for each. The row a single ACTIVATE opens spans every device on the sub-channel, so the effective open row is several KB.
  • On-die ECC: 128 data bits + 8 parity bits = a 136-bit codeword, Hamming SEC. This is not system ECC — it corrects in-device bit errors silently and does not surface to the OS.

Total bank parallelism therefore scales with channels × sub-channels × ranks × banks, and not at all with capacity. Replacing 2×32 GB with 1×64 GB can halve your rank count and measurably worsen random-access throughput while the "amount of RAM" is unchanged.

Refresh: the tax you cannot opt out of

DRAM cells leak. JEDEC DDR5 specifies tREFI = 3.9 µs for 0 °C ≤ Tcase < 85 °C and tREFI = 1.95 µs for 85 °C < Tcase ≤ 95 °C — the refresh rate doubles above 85 °C. Each all-bank refresh (REFab) blocks the device for tRFC1:

DensitytRFC1 (REFab)tRFC2tRFCsb (same-bank)

8 Gb195 ns130 ns115 ns
16 Gb295 ns160 ns130 ns

At 16 Gb and 3.9 µs, that is 295 / 3900 ≈ 7.6% of wall-clock time with the device unavailable — and roughly 15% once the DIMM crosses 85 °C. DDR5 adds same-bank refresh (REFsb), which requires only one bank per bank group to be idle instead of the whole device; Micron measures a 6–9% system throughput increase versus REFab, and average idle latency dropping from 11.2 ns to 5.0 ns on a 16 Gb device.

The same tREFI knob is also the front-line Rowhammer mitigation: tripling the refresh rate stops the current DDR5 attacks at a single-digit throughput cost, which is the security counterpart to everything above — see Rowhammer — DRAM bit flips, why TRR and ECC keep failing.

Operationally: a cooling problem presents as a memory-bandwidth problem. If bandwidth degrades under sustained load with no code change, check DIMM temperature before you check anything else.

Measuring it: activate rate and row-buffer hit ratio

You cannot infer row-buffer behaviour from cache-miss counters — an LLC miss tells you the request reached the memory controller, not what it cost there. You need uncore/memory-controller counters.

Intel (integrated memory controller, uncore_imc)

The relevant events on Ice Lake-SP and later:

EventCode / UMaskMeaning

UNC_M_ACT_COUNT.ALL0x01 / 0x0bACTIVATE commands issued
UNC_M_PRE_COUNT.PAGE_MISS0x02 / 0x0cPRECHARGEs caused by a row conflict
UNC_M_PRE_COUNT.ALL0x02 / 0x1call PRECHARGEs
UNC_M_CAS_COUNT.RD0x04 / 0x0fDRAM reads
UNC_M_CAS_COUNT.WR0x04 / 0x30DRAM writes

John McCalpin's decomposition (Intel Community, still the canonical formulation):

PCT_REQUESTS_PAGE_EMPTY = (ACT_COUNT - PRE_COUNT.PAGE_MISS) / (CAS_COUNT.RD + CAS_COUNT.WR)
PCT_REQUESTS_PAGE_MISS  = PRE_COUNT.PAGE_MISS            / (CAS_COUNT.RD + CAS_COUNT.WR)
PCT_REQUESTS_PAGE_HIT   = 1 - PCT_REQUESTS_PAGE_EMPTY - PCT_REQUESTS_PAGE_MISS


# Discover the exact PMU names on your platform first — they differ per generation.
perf list | grep -i 'unc_m_\(act\|pre\|cas\)_count'

sudo perf stat -a \
  -e 'uncore_imc/unc_m_act_count.all/' \
  -e 'uncore_imc/unc_m_pre_count.page_miss/' \
  -e 'uncore_imc/unc_m_cas_count.rd/' \
  -e 'uncore_imc/unc_m_cas_count.wr/' \
  -- ./your_workload

AMD (Zen 4 unified memory controller, amd_umc)

Linux 6.7 added the UMC PMU for family 19h. It exposes ACTIVATE, PRECHARGE and CAS counts:

ls /sys/bus/event_source/devices/amd_umc_*

# aggregate across all UMCs
sudo perf stat -a -e amd_umc/umc_act_cmd.all/ -e amd_umc/umc_cas_cmd.all/ -- sleep 10

# or per memory controller instance
sudo perf stat -a -e amd_umc_0/umc_cas_cmd.all/ -e amd_umc_4/umc_cas_cmd.all/ -- sleep 10

Available events: umc_mem_clk, umc_act_cmd.{all,rd,wr}, umc_pchg_cmd.{all,rd,wr}, umc_cas_cmd.{all,rd,wr}, umc_data_slot_clks.{all,rd,wr}.

The one metric worth tracking

ACT / CAS — activates per column access. It works identically on both vendors and needs no vendor-specific decomposition:

  • → 1.0 — every access opens a new row. Pointer chasing, hash probes, random gather. Worst case.
  • → 0 — many columns read per opened row. Sequential streaming, tiled loops.

The absolute value is platform-dependent (it depends on how many consecutive cache lines the address mapping keeps inside one row). Establish your own baseline: run a known-sequential memcpy-style loop over a buffer several times the LLC size, record its ACT/CAS, then compare your real workload against that number. The ratio between the two is meaningful even when the absolute value is not.

For a black-box picture without counters, Intel Memory Latency Checker (MLC) v3.12 (updated 2025-10-07) gives idle-latency and bandwidth matrices, cache-to-cache transfer latencies, and a loaded-latency sweep. Note that when it cannot control the hardware prefetchers it silently switches to random access for latency measurement — which changes what you are measuring. --loaded_latency is the mode that exposes bank/queue pressure; --latency_matrix is the NUMA question, covered separately in NUMA tuning — numactl, first-touch, and the remote-memory penalty.

Why you cannot compute your bank from a pointer

The physical-address → (channel, rank, bank group, bank, row, column) mapping is not documented by Intel or AMD, is XOR-based over physical address bits, and changes between microarchitectures. The DRAMA work (Pessl et al., USENIX Security 2016) reverse-engineered it precisely because it is undocumented — "Intel has changed the mapping used in its more recent microarchitectures" — and their method requires only that "the addressing functions are linear, i.e., they are XORs of physical-address bits."

The general layout: low bits are the byte index within the word, then column bits, one bit in between for channel selection, then bank/rank/DIMM bits, and the remaining upper bits select the row. The reverse-engineering primitive is exactly the timing gap this entry is about — DRAMA separated row hits from row conflicts on Ivy Bridge at a threshold "from 180 to 216 cycles row hits occur, but no row conflicts," and measured roughly 200–250 cycles for hits versus 300+ for conflicts on Haswell.

Three practical consequences:

1. A virtual address tells you nothing. You need the physical address (root, /proc/self/pagemap) and the undocumented mapping function. Do not write code that assumes a bank layout.
2. Huge pages give you contiguity, not the mapping. A 2 MB page is physically contiguous, so relative bank distribution within it becomes deterministic — that plus the reduced page-walk traffic is the real win. See TLB misses and page-table walks — huge pages, PCID, and shootdowns; note that page-table walks are themselves DRAM accesses and compete for the same banks.
3. The timing gap is a side channel. Row-buffer state is shared across cores and across VMs on the same DIMM, which is what made DRAMA a cross-CPU covert channel rather than a microbenchmarking curiosity.

What actually helps

Reduce the number of DRAM accesses first. Tiling/blocking so the working set fits in LLC beats every DRAM-level trick. The cheapest access is the one you do not make.

Avoid large power-of-two strides. Because the mapping XORs a small number of address bits, a stride of 4096, 8192, 65536… concentrates accesses onto a handful of banks and channels. Padding the leading dimension of a 2D array by one cache line (stride = N*sizeof(T) + 64) is the standard fix and often produces a step-function improvement in a matrix transpose or stencil loop.

Increase memory-level parallelism deliberately. Several independent sequential streams keep many banks open simultaneously and hide tRC behind each other. The prefetcher does this for you when the pattern is detectable; it does not when you chase pointers. Note the inflection point — beyond roughly the number of available banks, additional streams start evicting each other's open rows and the ACT/CAS ratio climbs back toward 1.0.

Populate channels and ranks, not capacity. Bank parallelism scales with channels × sub-channels × ranks. Two DIMMs per channel gives more ranks than one, though many platforms drop the data rate at 2 DPC — measure both configurations rather than assuming.

Fix NUMA first. Cross-socket traffic is a larger, more easily fixed effect than bank conflicts, and it changes which memory controller serves the request in the first place. Get first-touch placement right before tuning access patterns. Tiered and CXL-attached memory adds another latency class on top of this; see CXL — coherent memory pooling, tiering, and memory expansion.

Common gotchas

  • perf list shows the events but perf stat returns <not supported>. Uncore PMUs are per-socket and per-controller (uncore_imc_0 … uncore_imc_N, amd_umc_0 … amd_umc_N). Counting via the generic uncore_imc/…/ alias silently aggregates on some kernels and fails on others. When in doubt, enumerate /sys/bus/event_source/devices/ and sum the instances yourself. Uncore counting also requires -a (system-wide) — it cannot be attached to a single process, so you must isolate the machine.
  • Intel's ACT/PRE counters miss PRECHARGE ALL. McCalpin's caveat: the events "don't count precharges that come from the 'PRECHARGE ALL' command," which is issued during refresh and power-state transitions. The row-hit percentage is therefore slightly optimistic. It is infrequent enough not to matter for comparisons, and wrong enough that you should not publish the absolute number as exact.
  • AMD's umc_pchg_cmd does not split page-miss from page-close. There is no AMD equivalent of PRE_COUNT.PAGE_MISS, so the empty-vs-conflict decomposition is Intel-only. Use ACT/CAS on AMD.
  • On-die ECC hides errors from EDAC. DDR5's 136-bit on-die codeword corrects single-bit errors inside the device without reporting them. A DIMM that is degrading can look perfectly healthy in edac-util / rasdaemon until it exceeds on-die correction. On-die ECC is not a substitute for system ECC and does not make a UDIMM an ECC DIMM.
  • Above 85 °C the refresh rate doubles and bandwidth silently drops. tREFI goes 3.9 µs → 1.95 µs. Sustained-load benchmarks that "get slower after 10 minutes" are frequently this and not thermal throttling of the CPU.
  • More GB is not more bandwidth. Capacity per DIMM is orthogonal to channel and rank count. Consolidating onto fewer, larger DIMMs to free slots reduces bank parallelism.
  • A stride that is a large power of two is the classic pathology. It aliases in the cache and concentrates on few banks; the two effects compound, which is why the measured slowdown is often worse than either model predicts alone.
  • Do not measure with THP left at its default without checking. Huge pages change both the TLB behaviour and the physical contiguity your benchmark sees, so a run with and without them measures two different machines. Databases routinely disable THP for latency reasons — see Transparent huge pages and database latency — so a benchmark tuned with THP on may not describe your production host.
  • Row-buffer state leaks across security boundaries. If you are reasoning about co-tenancy, treat DRAM timing as observable by anything sharing the DIMM, including other VMs.
