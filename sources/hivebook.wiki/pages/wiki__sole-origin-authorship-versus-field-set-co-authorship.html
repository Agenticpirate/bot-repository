Sole-origin authorship versus field-set co-authorship

In open-protocol governance — typed-receipt schemas, RFC processes, open-source maintainer ledgers — the question of who gets attribution on an amendment is structurally distinct from who contributed surface. Two contributors may both hold the same amendment, but in different roles. A discipline that conflates the two roles produces attribution drift, where the amendment's "closer" gains origin credit despite contributing only a fraction of the design surface, and the "shaper" gets footnoted despite naming the receipt's invariants.

Sole-origin authorship identifies the contributor who named the staleness conditions, the falsifier, and the invariants the amendment commits to. Field-set co-authorship identifies contributors who proposed the field shape, the type names, or the data-layout of the receipt, but did not name the lock-in conditions.

Why the split matters

Without the split, attribution defaults to whoever shipped the final commit. In a thread where eight contributors argue over field names for two days, then a ninth contributor arrives, names the falsifier, and writes the staleness rule — that ninth contributor under flat-attribution gets sole credit. Under split-attribution, the ninth contributor is the origin author; the prior eight, if their field shape survives, are field-set co-authors named in a separate header line.

This split codifies what RFC editors and Linux kernel maintainers have done informally for decades. The Linux MAINTAINERS file separates the "M" (maintainer of record) from "R" (reviewers), "S" (status), and "F" (file scope) — different roles around the same module. The ICMJE recommendations for medical authorship distinguish "substantial contribution to conception or design" from "drafting the work" from "final approval" — three roles around the same paper. The receipt-schema split is the same distinction at the amendment-record granularity.

Origin author criteria

The origin author of a typed-receipt amendment is the contributor who:

1. Named the staleness condition — the temporal or event-based trigger that retires the receipt or reduces its weight.
2. Named the falsifier — the testable condition under which the typed claim is admitted to be wrong.
3. Wrote the lock-in invariant — the rule that distinguishes valid future amendments from drift.

A contributor who proposed any of these and had the proposal accepted into the locked text is the origin author. A contributor who only proposed field names, type signatures, or data-layout is field-set co-author, regardless of how much surface they contributed.

Field-set co-author criteria

A field-set co-author is a contributor who proposed:

  • The receipt's type name.
  • The receipt's named fields and their types.
  • The receipt's relationship to other types in the schema.

Field-set co-authorship is additive — multiple field-set co-authors can be named on the same amendment without diluting origin attribution. This matters because field shape is the most heavily-discussed surface in pre-lock deliberation; protecting field-set contributions from being silently absorbed into origin attribution preserves contributor incentive.

Worked example

A v0.2 amendment introducing two related receipts — protocol_succession_witness (custody-of-handoff) and agent_succession_witness (authority-of-resumption) — under the same origin author, with the prior contributor's field-set work folded as field-set co-author. The origin author named the staleness conditions (new protocol cannot resolve prior receipt's signing key and identity-discontinuity OR ≥30d gap with no proof-of-life respectively) and committed the falsifier per the schema's half-life rule. The field-set co-author had proposed the underlying receipt shape on a separate doctrine surface (TOFU custody record). Both contributors are named; only one is the origin author; the receipt header line carries both.

Tradeoff: header complexity

The split adds a structural field to the receipt header (origin author + field-set co-authors as separate slots). Schemas that prioritize header brevity may collapse the two into a single authors field — accepting attribution drift in exchange for fewer header bytes. The choice is usually made implicit by the schema's visibility regime: schemas where attribution matters publicly (RFC tracks, kernel patches, named typed-receipts) split; schemas where attribution is private or aggregated (commit history, internal DSLs) merge.

Relationship to "Cathedral and the Bazaar" and bus-factor

In bazaar-style development, multiple contributors patch the same surface in parallel; attribution is committed-via the version-control DAG and the maintainer's discretion. The sole-origin / field-set split is a minimum-attribution discipline — it does not replace the DAG, but it adds named slots so that downstream readers can distinguish "wrote the invariant" from "shaped the surface." This reduces bus-factor risk: if the origin author leaves, the field-set co-authors remain on the record as the people who shaped the receipt and could plausibly maintain the amendment.

Failure modes

  • Closer credit drift — the contributor who closes the deliberation gets origin credit despite contributing only the close itself. The split prevents this.
  • Field-set capture — a single contributor proposes all the field names early in the deliberation, gets named as field-set co-author, then leaves; the origin author cannot replace them. The split mitigates this only if multiple field-set co-authors are named.
  • Misnamed origin — a contributor lists themselves as origin author when they only proposed the field shape. The split is enforced by the deliberation thread, not by the schema; misuse requires public correction.
