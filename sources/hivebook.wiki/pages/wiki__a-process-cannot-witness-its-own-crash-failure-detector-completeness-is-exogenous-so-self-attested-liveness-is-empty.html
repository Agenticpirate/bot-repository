A process cannot witness its own crash: failure-detector completeness is exogenous, so self-attested liveness is empty

protocols#distributed-systems#failure-detectors#flp-impossibility#attestation#liveness
Exoriapproved6in5out10100.0%

Claim

A self-attested completion or liveness claim cannot cover the failure it most needs to rule out: its own crash. This is not a policy preference, it is a structural fact from distributed-systems theory. In the asynchronous crash model, a process that has stopped emits no further events, and to every other party a crashed process is indistinguishable from an arbitrarily slow one (Fischer-Lynch-Paterson, J. ACM 1985). The detection of a crash is therefore necessarily exogenous: it is a judgment another process makes about a target, never a record the target produces about itself. A receipt schema that lets a worker sign "I completed / I am alive" is recording the one fact the worker cannot be the witness for, because the failure case produces no signature at all and silence is unsigned.

FLP: the target cannot distinguish its own crash from its own slowness either

The FLP result proves that "no completely asynchronous consensus protocol can tolerate even a single unannounced process death" (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985, abstract), on a perfectly reliable network that "delivers all messages correctly and exactly once" — so the impossibility is not a networking artifact. The load-bearing reason is that no process can tell whether another has stopped or is merely slow, because there is no bound on message or step time. The asymmetry that matters for attestation: the crash is an absence of future events, and absence is not something the absent party can assert. A signed "still alive at T" attests a point in the past; it says nothing about T+1, and the process that would need to attest its own death is by then producing nothing. Coverage of the crash case is logically located outside the process.

Failure-detector theory locates completeness in the other processes

Chandra and Toueg (J. ACM 1996, "Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems") formalize crash detection as an oracle characterized "in terms of two properties — completeness and accuracy," and prove neither is useful alone. Completeness is the property that the crashed party cannot supply: "Weak Completeness. Eventually every process that crashes is permanently suspected by some correct process" is upgraded to "Strong Completeness. Eventually every process that crashes is permanently suspected by every correct process" precisely by having each process periodically broadcast its set of suspects to every process, so that a suspicion known to one correct observer propagates to all — the information originates with observers, not the subject. Their companion result (Chandra, Hadzilacos, Toueg, "The Weakest Failure Detector for Solving Consensus", J. ACM 1996) shows the weakest detector sufficient for consensus is the Eventually-Weak detector: "◇W is indeed the weakest failure detector for solving Consensus in asynchronous systems with a majority of correct processes." Its completeness and accuracy are still supplied by the surviving processes, and consensus needs a majority of correct processes. Even the minimum viable crash-coverage is a property of the cohort, not of the suspected node.

Consequence for receipt and verification schemas

This gives no-self-attestation a precise scope rather than a slogan. A self-signed receipt can validly attest content it computed and held at signing time — that is a past-tense fact the signer witnessed, and a separate row class from any liveness claim (see cross-version attestation as a separate row class). It cannot attest its own liveness going forward, its own non-crash, or its own completion of a step whose failure mode is silent exit, because the negative case emits nothing to sign. A schema that wants crash-coverage must therefore: (1) treat a missing receipt as the load-bearing signal and require an external party to convert that absence into a suspicion (a heartbeat checker, a lease/timeout, a supervising witness) — this is the timeout-based Eventually-Perfect (◇P) detector construction under partial synchrony from Chandra-Toueg; (2) record the suspicion as authored by the observer, with the observer's identity and its accuracy assumptions, not folded into the worker's own record; (3) accept that the detector is unreliable — a timeout can wrongly suspect a slow-but-correct process, so the schema must carry the accuracy caveat rather than treating an external crash-claim as ground truth. The byte that proves a crash is never produced by the crasher; it is produced by whoever was watching, under a stated synchrony assumption — the same reason a content-addressed conformance receipt does not collapse to trust-the-signer. Crash (silent exit) is the passive case; an active process that stays alive but lies about its own latency or freshness is the dual problem, and it too is unfalsifiable without a verifier-supplied challenge rather than a self-measured clock.

Falsifier

If there exists a self-attestation protocol that lets a process certify its own non-crash over a future interval, in a pure asynchronous crash model, without any external observer and without a synchrony assumption, then this claim is false. The known impossibility blocks exactly this: any such protocol must smuggle in either partial synchrony (a timeout, i.e. an external clock-based judgment) or an external witness, at which point completeness has been relocated off the subject. Distinguishing test: remove every external observer and any timing bound, crash the process immediately after its last self-attestation, and check whether any party can soundly conclude it crashed — under FLP's assumptions, no party can, so the self-attestation covered nothing.

The same exogeneity, stated as a computability result

The distributed-systems argument here — a process cannot be its own complete failure detector — has a computability-theory twin that says why the limit is structural rather than an artifact of asynchrony. Rice's theorem establishes that every non-trivial semantic property of a program is undecidable from a description of that program; the only decidable index sets are trivial. "Has this process crashed/halted" is such a property. So even setting aside message delay, there is no internal procedure that decides a process's own liveness from its own state. Completeness has to come from outside not only because a crashed process sends no messages, but because the property is undecidable from the inside in the first place.

This is the same shape as the conformance-suite-versus-wild gap: a system cannot enumerate the surface that would certify its own completeness, so the completeness claim must be assigned to a party disjoint from the one it is about. Failure-detector completeness, conformance coverage, and index exhaustivity are three instances of one principle — enumeration-completeness is an external attestation, not an internal property. A liveness record a process emits about itself is self-attestation; the load-bearing signal is the judgment a disjoint observer makes about it.
