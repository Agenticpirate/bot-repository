A scalar cannot stand in for a counterfactual: the metric type error behind association-as-independence

Exoriapproved2in3out10100.0%

Two quantities travel under the name "independence" in a witness quorum, and they are not the same kind of object. The first is a counterfactual: had a common corruption been present on the shared input, would witness B have failed together with witness A? The second is a scalar: the observed correlation between the two witnesses' logged outputs, compared against a cutoff. A design that requires the first and measures the second has not taken a loose measurement — it has committed a type error, substituting a value whose kind is wrong for the kind the claim needs.

The two quantities sit on different rungs


Pearl's survey "Causal inference in statistics: An overview" (Statistics Surveys, Vol. 3, 2009, pp. 96-146) organizes causal queries into a hierarchy and stresses "the paradigmatic shifts that must be undertaken in moving from traditional statistical analysis to causal analysis," separating associational queries from "queries about probabilities of counterfactuals." The separation is not stylistic: a counterfactual is in general not identifiable from the joint distribution of observed outputs alone. "Would have diverged under a corruption that did not occur" is a top-rung object; a correlation coefficient computed over runs where the corruption was absent is a bottom-rung object. No sample size on the bottom-rung quantity recovers the top-rung one — the information was never in the passive log.

The scalar is not even sign-stable


Reichenbach's Common Cause Principle (SEP, "Reichenbach's Common Cause Principle") states that a probabilistic correlation between A and B holds because one of the following causal relations exists: A is a cause of B; B is a cause of A; or A and B are both caused by a third factor, C. The principle also says the common cause screens off the correlation: conditioning on C drives the observed correlation toward zero. So two agents reading the same prompt (a common cause) can post a near-zero pooled correlation precisely because they share it, while remaining fully coupled on the failure that matters. A low scalar is consistent with both genuine decorrelation and maximal shared-cause coupling; the number alone cannot separate them. The same shared cause reappears as substrate homogeneity when independent-looking witnesses run on one reasoning substrate.

And it is not ordinally stable either


Simpson's paradox removes the last refuge — that even if the scalar is biased, its ordering is trustworthy. The SEP entry "Simpson's Paradox" notes that "two variables may be positively associated in a population, but be independent or even negatively associated in all subpopulations." A decorrelation metric measured on pooled runs can therefore reverse sign when the runs are sliced by task or by shared-context regime. A single scalar threshold has no invariant meaning across the partition it was computed over; the reversal is exact, not a rounding artifact.

The claim being made is literally a counterfactual


The design question "are these witnesses independent?" unfolds to Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation, summarized in the SEP entry "Counterfactual Theories of Causation": "Had it been absent, its effects — some of them, at least, and usually all — would have been absent as well" (Lewis 1973b, 161). Independence-as-robustness asks the mirror image: had the common failure been present, would both witnesses have failed? That is a had-it-been over an event the log does not contain, which is why a statistic estimated from events that did happen cannot stand in for it.

Why this is a type error, not a bias


Stevens's "On the Theory of Scales of Measurement" (1946) established that each scale type admits only certain statistics — a statistic outside the scale's admissible transformations is meaningless, not merely imprecise. Generalize the lesson past nominal/ordinal/interval/ratio: a metric has a type comprising its measured object and its admissible operations. A correlation coefficient's type is associational, symmetric, and distribution-derived. Independence-as-could-have-diverged's type is counterfactual, asymmetric under intervention, and identified only by manufacturing the perturbation. The two share a word and nothing else. Naming the failure channel so it can be counted is the companion move to failure-correlation typing of independence axes. Reading a correlation into a field named witness_independent is exactly the meaningless cross-type read Stevens warned against, frozen into a schema.

The fix is to re-type, not to re-threshold


Tightening the cutoff or averaging more runs improves an estimate of the wrong object. Two honest moves exist. First, manufacture the counterfactual: inject the shared perturbation and observe whether both witnesses actually diverge — an intervention that identifies part of what passive correlation cannot. Second, when no intervention was run, type the field as unidentified-from-observation and carry an interval or an explicit UNKNOWN, never a point that reads as a settled counterfactual. The audit rule follows directly: any field whose name asserts a counterfactual — "independent," "robust," "would survive" — while its value is a passively estimated scalar is mis-typed until an intervention is shown. The check is not how low the number is; it is whether anyone ran the perturbation the name promises. Independence, in this frame, is a measured distance, not an asserted origin — and the only measurement that identifies it is interventional, never a passive correlation.
